Single-event hydrological modelling of the Anambra River using HEC-HMS

Rainfall-runoff simulation · flood risk mapping
Simulation windowJun 10–15, 2025
Elements modelled22 subbasins, 10 reaches
Peak discharge1,804.5 m³/s
SoftwareHEC-HMS 4.13, QGIS

Overview

Anambra State is one of Nigeria's highest flood-risk zones — 37 locations across the state were flood-affected between October and November 2024 alone. This project models the Anambra River Basin, a roughly 3,000 km² catchment within the wider Niger Basin system, to understand how a single intense storm event moves through the watershed and where the resulting flood risk concentrates.

The goal was threefold: determine peak flow rates across the basin's hydrological elements, map the timing of peak flow arrivals to understand how the flood wave travels downstream, and calculate runoff volumes by drainage area to characterise the basin's overall hydrological response.

Methodology

Data & inputs

  • Topography: 30m-resolution SRTM digital elevation model, used to delineate watershed boundaries, subbasins, and the river network
  • Rainfall: Daily precipitation from NASA POWER, Jan 1 – Jun 10, 2025
  • Runoff response: SCS Curve Number method (CN values 89–91 across subbasins) combined with the SCS Unit Hydrograph for rainfall-to-runoff transformation

Model structure

The basin model was built from DEM-derived topography into 22 subbasins, 9 routing reaches, and 1 sink, each parameterised for loss (SCS Curve Number), transform (SCS Unit Hydrograph), and baseflow. Channel routing between elements used the Muskingum method. The simulation ran at a 2-minute timestep across a 5-day window, June 10–15, 2025, with results processed and mapped in QGIS.

Results

The basin outlet at Otuocha recorded the highest peak discharge in the system — 1,804.5 m³/s at 11:42am on June 14, arriving roughly 4 hours after the upstream peaks began cascading through the network. Reach-6, near the Ezu–Owoleka–Nnem confluence, followed closely at 1,573.7 m³/s.

Upstream subbasins such as Subbasin-16 (Ukukwa) and Subbasin-21 (Ajalli) peaked first, as early as 8:24–8:38am, with the flood wave progressing downstream to the major reaches between 11:16am and 11:56am. Subbasin-9 (Amaetiti) contributed the highest subbasin-level discharge at 197.8 m³/s, showing a sharp rise-and-fall hydrograph characteristic of fast-responding, low-infiltration catchments — a flash-flood signature.

Why it matters

Hydrographs this sharp — rapid rise, short peak window, steep recession — indicate limited infiltration and a basin that leaves very little lead time for emergency response. The results support three practical uses: sizing hydraulic infrastructure like bridges and culverts for high-flow resilience, delineating floodplains to flag inundation-prone areas, and informing early-warning thresholds and land-use zoning in the highest-risk reaches identified here.
